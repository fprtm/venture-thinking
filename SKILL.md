# Venture Thinking

An idea is not a plan — it is a bundle of **assumptions** that are still untested. This skill runs the loop that turns an idea into a **hypothesis**, finds the one assumption most likely to be wrong, and designs the **cheapest test** that could kill it. The person leaves with a bet they can act on this week, not a plan they will polish for a month.

Amateurs ask *"is my idea good?"* This loop asks *"which assumption is riskiest, and what is the cheapest way to find out?"*

## Stance

Hold these while running the loop — they override the default urge to help build:

- **Idea → hypothesis.** Restate every idea as a claim that can be proven wrong. "An app for X" is not testable; "clinic owners lose 3 hours a day to manual WhatsApp orders and will pay Rp1jt/mo to stop" is.
- **Refuse the solution jump.** When someone leads with a solution ("I'll build a CRM"), pull back to the problem first. The solution is a guess about a problem; name the problem before defending the guess.
- **Hunt the riskiest assumption, not confirmation.** Your job is to find the belief that, if false, sinks the whole thing — then attack *that*, cheaply, before anything gets built.
- **Painkiller over vitamin.** A painkiller is something the customer *must* have; a vitamin is nice-to-have. Only painkillers sell easily. Grade the pain honestly.
- **Match rigor to reversibility.** A **reversible** decision (copy, pricing test, MVP scope) is cheap to undo — decide fast and test. An **irreversible** one (big hire, capital, long contract, new market) is expensive to undo — that is where deep reasoning earns its keep. Do not deep-think reversible bets; do not gamble irreversible ones.
- **Close the gap, don't just point at it.** Flagging a risk is half the job. Before handing back a "go find out" homework assignment, spend your own research budget trying to shrink it: desk research *and* social listening (Reddit, X, community groups, app-store/review-site complaints — searched by you, now) for this exact customer in their own words. Only ask the person to act on what's left — specifically, willingness-to-pay, which no search can answer (see [`references/research.md`](references/research.md)).
- **Propose, don't just object.** If a stronger wedge, cheaper channel, better price point, or an adjacent problem with better economics is visible, say so explicitly with reasoning — do not just critique their idea and stop. Skepticism without a counter-offer is a commissioner's job, not this skill's.
- **We're searching for a painful problem with a reachable, budgeted buyer — not "something I can build."** Technology is the implementation vehicle, chosen after the pain and buyer are real, never the starting point. Never open with "what app/SaaS/AI product should this be" (see [`references/discovery.md`](references/discovery.md) if there's no idea yet at all).
- **Follow the money and ladder the pain to its real consequence.** Name the economic buyer, the user, the champion, and the blocker separately (they're often different people) — an opportunity with no identifiable budget owner gets aggressively downgraded. And never stop at the surface complaint: keep asking "why does that matter?" until it terminates in an economic, operational, regulatory, or reputational consequence — that deeper consequence, not the surface annoyance, is what's actually sellable. Detail: [`references/red-team.md`](references/red-team.md).
- **Never stop at "kill it."** The loop isn't done until you've hunted for the sharper version. If the idea survives testing, name the gap it still doesn't cover and propose the most inventive fix you can find for it — a wedge, a feature, a channel, a business-model twist — not just a pass/fail stamp. If the test has already killed it (or clearly will), don't stop at red: use the same research to pin down exactly what killed it, then propose one concrete pivot that removes that specific flaw and would plausibly survive a retest. "It's dead" is never the final artifact — "here's the version that isn't" is. Then actually run that pivot back through the loop — see **Iterate** below; a pivot that only exists as a suggested paragraph hasn't been tested at all.
- **Ground the idea in real domain vocabulary, not invented placeholders.** Before finalizing FRAME or naming assumptions, check the idea against real industry/role/process knowledge instead of guessing generically — see [`references/domain-knowledge.md`](references/domain-knowledge.md). This is vocabulary only, never evidence of real-world conditions — see the mandatory internet-research rule below.

## Rigor tiers: SNIFF vs FULL — don't spend full weight on every round

A real run of this skill applied full weight (50-source TEST, complete Bull/Bear/Kill-Shot writeup) to *every* round of *every* candidate, including early, cheaply-reversible ones — 10 candidates and 12 cumulative rounds later, zero survived, at a cost that a 10-minute check would have avoided for several of them (see the named traps in [`references/red-team.md`](references/red-team.md) — most of those deaths were cheap to see coming). This is the fix: two tiers, escalate only what earns it.

- **SNIFF** *(default for: every DISCOVER industry/segment/niche pick, and rounds 1–2 of any pivot in Iterate)* — quick reasoning + ~10–15 sources, checked against the five named traps and a one-line falsification gate ([`references/red-team.md`](references/red-team.md)). No full Bull/Bear/Kill-Shot writeup, no 50-source minimum, no CANVAS. This is meant to kill obviously-dead candidates in minutes, not hours.
- **FULL** *(only for: the single opportunity DISCOVER hands to FRAME, and any round that survives a SNIFF pass)* — everything already specified per-stage below: complete RED TEAM writeup, 50-source TEST, CANVAS.

**Escalation rule, not a free choice:** a round always starts at SNIFF. If it dies there, log it and move on — cheap. If it survives SNIFF, escalate that same round to FULL before committing to BET/CANVAS. Never skip SNIFF to jump straight to FULL "to save a step" — that's exactly the shortcut that let the real failure case burn 50 sources on candidates a 15-source check would have killed. The Iteration Log's **Tier** column (below) makes which tier each round actually got auditable, not asserted.

## The loop

Locate where the person is, run that stage, then push to the next. Do not run stages they are past.

0. **DISCOVER** *(only when there is no idea yet)* — Runs entirely at **SNIFF tier** through all its narrowing steps. If the person wants a market/pain found from scratch rather than an existing idea tested, narrow top-down, one decision at a time: pick the single best **industry** first → **segment** that industry's market → pick the single best **niche** within it → only then hunt for **pain** inside that niche (workflow map, workarounds, competition, buying signals) → market-map table of candidate pains → pain-score → shortlist ~5 → deep-dive the top 3 → pick one. Full process: [`references/discovery.md`](references/discovery.md). **Done when:** one opportunity is picked (with a stated second/third choice and why they lost), and it's handed to FRAME as the hypothesis to interrogate — that hand-off is the moment SNIFF escalates to FULL for the chosen candidate. Skip this stage entirely if they already brought an idea.

1. **FRAME** — Force the problem into focus before any solution. Answer, out loud, for their idea: *who exactly*, *what problem*, *why it happens*, *how often*, *how painful*, *what they use today*, *will they pay*. Vague answers here are the finding — chase them down; ground the customer/role/process in real domain knowledge rather than a generic guess ([`references/domain-knowledge.md`](references/domain-knowledge.md)). Full battery: [`references/framing.md`](references/framing.md). **Done when:** the idea is stated as one falsifiable hypothesis sentence naming the customer, the pain, and the willingness-to-pay.

2. **ASSUME** — List everything that must be true for this to work (customer has the problem / it is painful enough / they will pay $X / you can reach them profitably / you can deliver it / it can get large). Rank each by *risk × uncertainty*. Cross-check against the domain's real value chain ([`references/domain-knowledge.md`](references/domain-knowledge.md)) — an assumption that skips a layer of who-does-what-with-what is usually the unexamined one. **Done when:** the single riskiest assumption is named — the one that is both most damaging if false and least known today.

3. **RED TEAM** — **Start at SNIFF**: check the hypothesis against the five named traps and state a one-line falsification condition ([`references/red-team.md`](references/red-team.md)) — this alone kills a large share of dead-on-arrival hypotheses in minutes. Only if it survives that, **escalate to FULL**: run the complete objection list, name the economic buyer/user/champion/blocker, classify existing evidence (VERIFIED FACT → UNKNOWN), and write the Bull Case / Bear Case / Kill Shot / What-would-change-our-mind. **Done when:** either SNIFF killed it (cheap, logged, move on) or all four of Bull/Bear/Kill-Shot/What-would-change-our-mind are written with specifics at FULL — this is the concrete form of "never stop at kill it," not satisfied by a vague risk list.

4. **TEST** — **Start at SNIFF**: ~10–15 sources of quick desk research to see whether the named traps are confirmed or refuted and whether the pain shows up at all. If it dies here, that's a cheap kill — log it and move to the next pivot. If it survives, **escalate to FULL**: run desk research *and* social listening yourself, in this turn, up to the mandatory **50 distinct sources read and understood** (see [`references/research.md`](references/research.md) for what counts and how to report count/spread) — search Reddit, X, Facebook/community groups, App/Play Store and G2/Capterra reviews, and forums for this exact customer + problem in their own words. **The moment SNIFF clears, also start real outreach in parallel** — draft the outreach message and identify a handful of real prospects now, and have the person start sending it *while* the deeper desk research continues, rather than waiting for all 50 sources to finish first ([`references/research.md`](references/research.md)). Atlas grounding covers vocabulary, never market reality — it never counts toward either tier's source count. State plainly what the research did and didn't settle, and which stage of the [PMF ladder](references/red-team.md) the evidence actually supports. What's left after both run — specifically "will they pay this price for this offer" — needs a person-run test, the smallest one that can answer it: landing page + ad spend, pre-order/waitlist, concierge/manual MVP, pilot. Reach for open-ended interviews only if social listening found nothing usable, and even then scope it small (3–5 people, days not a week), with behavioral not hypothetical questions ([`references/research.md`](references/research.md)). Not a finished product. State the pass/fail signal *before* running it. **Done when:** either SNIFF killed it cheaply, or the 50-source minimum is met (or the shortfall is explained) at FULL, there is one test, its cost, its turnaround time, and the observable result that would confirm or refute the assumption. Stage map (BUILD→SELL→LEARN→SCALE) if they are further along: [`references/loop.md`](references/loop.md).

5. **BET** — Convert to one concrete Horizon-1 action for this week (e.g. "launch a landing page with two pricing tiers by Wednesday" or "interview 5 clinic owners by Friday" — whichever the TEST stage actually produced), while keeping the ambitious Horizon-3 vision as the North Star. Use the 7-day-execution-plan / first-customer-plan formats when the person is a solo/small builder starting from zero: [`references/loop.md`](references/loop.md). **Done when:** the person has a single next action with a deadline.

6. **CANVAS** *(FULL tier only)* — Once FRAME (and ideally ASSUME/TEST) have real material, produce a proper Business Model Canvas: 9 blocks, each pulled directly from what the loop already established, not invented fresh — see [`references/business-model-canvas.md`](references/business-model-canvas.md) for the block-by-block sourcing rules, what "generic vs. specific" looks like per block, and how to mark unvalidated blocks. Price Revenue Streams against the customer-economics cost-of-pain calculation ([`references/economics.md`](references/economics.md)), not a feature count. Skip this stage (or produce only a flagged draft) if FRAME alone isn't done yet — a canvas off a one-line pitch is fiction. Localize any global-research benchmarks back to the actual target market's pricing power, channels, payment habits, and regulation before they land in the canvas — a foreign data point calibrates, it doesn't get copied in verbatim (same reference file has the localization rules). **Done when:** all 9 blocks are filled with specifics traceable to FRAME/ASSUME/TEST/economics, internally consistent, and the least-validated block is named.

Close every session with seven artifacts: **the hypothesis**, **the riskiest assumption**, **the red team write-up** (Bull/Bear/Kill-Shot/what-would-change-our-mind + falsification conditions), **the cheapest test + its pass/fail signal**, **the one action this week**, **the sharper version** — if the idea is still alive, the gap it doesn't cover yet plus the most inventive fix for it ("no better angle found" is only valid after genuinely looking); if the test killed it (or clearly will), the exact cause of death plus one concrete pivot that removes it — and **the Business Model Canvas** (or an explicitly flagged draft if FRAME isn't done). If more than one round ran, add an eighth: **the Iteration Log** (see Iterate below) — the full round-by-round table, not a summary of it. If any is missing, the loop is not done.

## Iterate: this is a loop, not one pass

The person isn't selling a fixed product or service — they're selling **a validated solution to a problem**, and "validated" is the actual deliverable, not "one hypothesis tested." One FRAME→ASSUME→TEST→BET→CANVAS pass rarely produces a hypothesis that survives on the first try — most of the time RED TEAM or TEST kills it at SNIFF (cheap), or occasionally at FULL (more expensive, still far cheaper than building it). When that happens, don't stop at reporting it: **restart the loop at FRAME with the sharper version / pivot as the new hypothesis**, and run it through ASSUME→RED TEAM→TEST→BET→CANVAS again, starting back at **SNIFF** — a pivot doesn't inherit its predecessor's tier. Carry forward research already done (don't re-run the same sources if the customer/domain didn't change) and only do fresh research for what the pivot actually changed (new segment, new channel, new price). Because most rounds now die cheap at SNIFF, hitting the 5- or 10-round cap should cost a fraction of what it used to — if a session is burning full 50-source treatment on most rounds, that's a sign SNIFF isn't actually being run first.

**Stop iterating when one of these is true, and say explicitly which:**
- A hypothesis's riskiest assumption survives TEST (real evidence — social listening or a true-primary result — supports it, not just "no better angle found"). This is the success exit: report it as validated and move to BET/CANVAS as the final round.
- **Five iterations** have run and none survived — this is the mandatory diagnosis checkpoint (see below), not necessarily a full stop.
- **Ten iterations** have run and none survived — this is a **hard ceiling, no exceptions**. Stop, report the full iteration history (every hypothesis, what killed each one, what was learned across all of them), and say plainly that no validated angle was found within this session — don't quietly keep spinning or fake a pass to end the loop. **Zero surviving hypotheses is a legitimate, useful result, not a failed session** — the real deliverable at that point is the extracted cross-round pattern(s) (see below), which is worth more than one untested idea would have been.
- The riskiest remaining assumption is now **irreversible to test cheaply** (would require real capital, a long contract, or burning the only shot at this customer segment) — stop and flag that further iteration needs a different kind of decision, not another cheap test.
- The person explicitly says they want to commit and build on the current best hypothesis, even if unvalidated — respect that; don't keep re-litigating (see "When NOT to run this").

Each iteration's artifacts stay part of the record — don't discard round 1's findings when round 2 starts; a later pivot is informed by exactly what killed the earlier one.

**When the loop ends with zero survivors, extract the cross-round pattern before closing the session.** Scan the "Why" column of the Iteration Log for a shared root cause across rounds (a real DISCOVER run that killed 10/10 candidates found, e.g., that every failure traced back to one underlying constraint — see the named traps in [`references/red-team.md`](references/red-team.md) for concrete examples). That pattern — stated as a reusable filter for the *next* search, not just a postmortem of this one — is the actual deliverable of a session that ends at zero, and it's worth more than a single untested idea would have been. Don't close a zero-survivor session with just a list of deaths; close it with the rule those deaths imply.

### Iteration Log — a visible, running record of every round

Keep one running log, shown to the person and updated after every round (not written from memory at the very end) — it must be readable at a glance what each round *wanted to prove* and *why it failed or survived*:

| Round | Hypothesis (FRAME) | Riskiest Assumption | Tier | Stage Reached | Outcome | Why (cause of death, or why it survived) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ... | ... | SNIFF / FULL | RED TEAM / TEST / CANVAS | Killed / Survived | ... |
| 2 | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |

Rules for keeping it honest and actually useful:

- **Append, never overwrite.** Round 2's row doesn't replace round 1's — the whole table is the point, so a later pivot's reasoning ("this round is different because...") can be checked against what's actually in the table, not against a claim about it.
- **"Tier" says how expensive the round actually was** — SNIFF (10–15 sources, no full writeup) or FULL (50 sources, complete red-team writeup, canvas). A session with mostly-SNIFF rows is working as designed; a session with mostly-FULL rows on early pivots means the escalation rule isn't being followed.
- **"Stage Reached" says where it died**, not just "TEST" — a hypothesis can die at RED TEAM (didn't survive the falsification check before any research was spent), at TEST (research/evidence didn't support the assumption), or at CANVAS (a block turned out inconsistent). Naming the stage tells you how expensive the mistake was to catch.
- **"Why" must be the specific reason**, not a category label — "killed at TEST: 40+ sources found this exact pain, but zero evidence anyone pays for it (Level 2 evidence, never above)" is useful; "didn't validate" is not.
- **If DISCOVER (Stage 0) ran**, log its narrowing decisions too, in the same spirit: which industry/segment/niche was picked at each step and why it beat the alternatives — so if the DISCOVER-restart path triggers later (systemic-failure diagnosis at 5 iterations), the earlier industry/niche choices and why they were made are still visible, not re-litigated from scratch.
- **At the diagnosis checkpoints (5 and every round after)**, the systemic-vs-distinct read *is* a scan of this table's "Why" column across rounds — point at which rows share a root cause when calling it systemic, don't just assert it.
- **In the final report**, the full table is one of the required artifacts whenever more than one round ran — it's the evidence behind "sharper version" and behind any recommendation to stop or restart from DISCOVER, not an optional appendix.

### At 5 iterations: diagnose before continuing toward the cap of 10

Five was chosen deliberately: three is often too tight to tell whether pivoting is genuinely converging or just thrashing, and ten rounds of a 50-source TEST each is expensive enough that it shouldn't run unexamined. At 5, stop and read the iteration history: did each round die from a **different, unrelated** cause (bad channel, then bad price, then no urgency) — pivoting has genuinely been narrowing it down and a few more targeted rounds could plausibly land it? Or did every round die from **the same underlying reason wearing a different costume** (this customer doesn't have the pain / can't be reached profitably / this whole problem space is a vitamin, not a painkiller) — in which case more pivots inside the same idea are polishing a dead thing.

- **If the failures are related/systemic:** say so explicitly, name the shared root cause across all 5 rounds, and recommend **abandoning this problem/customer entirely and restarting from DISCOVER** ([`references/discovery.md`](references/discovery.md)) with a genuinely different problem or customer, not another variant of the same one — treat it as a fresh wide-funnel search (possibly in a different Atlas industry), not a FRAME-level tweak. Do this even though the hard ceiling isn't reached yet — grinding to 10 on a diagnosed-systemic failure just spends the remaining budget on a foregone conclusion.
- **If the failures are genuinely distinct:** it's fair to continue, but re-run this same diagnosis after each subsequent round (not just once at 5) up to the hard ceiling of 10, and say explicitly each time why the next round is different from the ones before it — don't continue on inertia alone.
- Either way, this is a recommendation to state plainly, not a decision to make unilaterally — hand it back to the person as "here's what I'd do next and why" and let them choose, including the option to stop short of 10 even when a systemic diagnosis hasn't been reached.

## Research: desk, social listening, and true primary

Use **web search / browser tools** during OBSERVE, FRAME, and TEST to run two kinds of research yourself, before assigning anything to the person: **desk research** (existing solutions, competitors, market size, pricing, trends) and **social listening** (searching Reddit, X, community groups, App/Play Store and review-site complaints for this exact customer talking about this exact pain, unprompted, in their own words). Together these settle most of what a first interview round exists to find — do them in the same turn you're asked, not as a suggestion.

This internet research is **mandatory for seeing real conditions** — it is how the loop knows what's actually happening in the market, not an optional enrichment step. Atlas Domain Knowledge is a separate, narrower tool: pull it in only when the loop needs real business/domain vocabulary (role names, process steps, system types) it doesn't already have — it is never a stand-in for checking real conditions, and never counts toward either tier's source count below.

**Two tiers, not one flat minimum** (see "Rigor tiers" above): a fast **SNIFF** pass (~10–15 sources) runs first on every hypothesis to check the named traps and whether the pain shows up at all; only a hypothesis that survives SNIFF escalates to the **FULL** bar of **at least 50 distinct websites/sources read and understood** (competitor pages, reviews, forum threads, marketplace listings, articles, social posts) across desk research + social listening combined. State which tier ran, the count, and the spread when reporting back. Full detail: [`references/research.md`](references/research.md).

Search **globally, not just the local market or local language** — run both kinds of research in English and other relevant languages against worldwide sources, not only Indonesian ones, even for an Indonesia-first idea. A sharper wedge, a cheaper channel, or a cautionary already-tried solution can come from anywhere.

**The moment a hypothesis clears SNIFF, start real outreach in parallel with the deeper FULL-tier research** — draft the message and identify a few real prospects now, don't wait for all 50 sources to finish before the person is allowed to talk to anyone. What's left after both run has a hard ceiling neither search can reach: whether this customer will actually *pay* this price for this offer. That's the only thing that needs a person-run test — and it should be the smallest one that answers just that question, not a default week of interviews. How to run each well: [`references/research.md`](references/research.md).

## Mode: single-session vs continuous

Two ways to run DISCOVER + Iterate, and the person should say (or be asked) which fits, since they cost very differently:

- **Single-session** *(default)* — the full wide-then-narrow DISCOVER funnel plus up to 10 iteration rounds, all in one sitting, aiming to reach a validated (or conclusively dead) hypothesis before the session ends. Right for a dedicated "let's find something worth building" push.
- **Continuous** *(opt in — the person says something like "cari ide pelan-pelan," "mode berkelanjutan," or wants to hunt for ideas as an ongoing habit rather than one marathon)* — mirrors Teresa Torres's continuous-discovery critique of big batch research sprints: run small, frequent, cheap passes instead of one exhaustive session. Each session brings only 1–3 candidate niches (skip the full 10–20 industry scan), runs them at SNIFF only, and escalates **at most one** to FULL before stopping — deliberately smaller than the single-session cap, meant to repeat across separate sessions/days rather than grind to round 10 in one sitting. The Iteration Log and Kill-pattern notes (dead arguments, named traps confirmed) carry over between sessions so later sessions don't re-litigate what an earlier one already settled.

Default to single-session unless the person signals otherwise; don't silently switch modes mid-session.

## When NOT to run this

- They have already validated and committed, and are now building — help build, do not re-litigate the idea.
- The decision is small and reversible — just make the call and move on; this loop is for bets worth reasoning about.
