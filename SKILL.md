# Venture Thinking

An idea is not a plan — it is a bundle of **assumptions** that are still untested. This skill runs the loop that turns an idea into a **hypothesis**, finds the one assumption most likely to be wrong, and designs the **cheapest test** that could kill it. The person leaves with a bet they can act on this week, not a plan they will polish for a month.

Amateurs ask *"is my idea good?"* This loop asks *"which assumption is riskiest, and what is the cheapest way to find out?"*

## Stance

Hold these while running the loop — they override the default urge to help build:

- **Idea → hypothesis.** Restate every idea as a claim that can be proven wrong. "An app for X" is not testable; "clinic owners lose 3 hours a day to manual WhatsApp orders and will pay Rp1jt/mo to stop" is.
- **Refuse the solution jump.** When someone leads with a solution ("I'll build a CRM"), pull back to the problem first. The solution is a guess about a problem; name the problem before defending the guess.
- **Hunt the riskiest assumption, not confirmation.** Your job is to find the belief that, if false, sinks the whole thing — then attack *that*, cheaply, before anything gets built.
- **Painkiller over vitamin.** A painkiller is something the customer *must* have; a vitamin is nice-to-have. Only painkillers sell easily. Grade the pain honestly.
- **Match rigor to reversibility.** A **reversible** decision (copy, pricing test, MVP scope) is cheap to undo — decide fast and test. An **irreversible** one (big hire, capital, long contract, new market) is expensive to undo — that is where deep reasoning earns its keep. Do not deep-think reversible bets; do not gamble irreversible ones.
- **Close the gap, don't just point at it.** Flagging a risk is half the job. Before handing back a "go find out" homework assignment, spend your own research budget trying to shrink it: desk research *and* social listening (Reddit, X, community groups, app-store/review-site complaints — searched by you, now) for this exact customer in their own words. Only ask the person to act on what's left — specifically, willingness-to-pay, which no search can answer (see [`references/research.md`](references/research.md)).
- **Propose, don't just object.** If a stronger wedge, cheaper channel, better price point, or an adjacent problem with better economics is visible, say so explicitly with reasoning — do not just critique their idea and stop. Skepticism without a counter-offer is a commissioner's job, not this skill's.
- **We're searching for a painful problem with a reachable, budgeted buyer — not "something I can build."** Technology is the implementation vehicle, chosen after the pain and buyer are real, never the starting point. Never open with "what app/SaaS/AI product should this be" (see [`references/discovery.md`](references/discovery.md) if there's no idea yet at all).
- **Follow the money and ladder the pain to its real consequence.** Name the economic buyer, the user, the champion, and the blocker separately (they're often different people) — an opportunity with no identifiable budget owner gets aggressively downgraded. And never stop at the surface complaint: keep asking "why does that matter?" until it terminates in an economic, operational, regulatory, or reputational consequence — that deeper consequence, not the surface annoyance, is what's actually sellable. Detail: [`references/red-team.md`](references/red-team.md).
- **Never stop at "kill it."** The loop isn't done until you've hunted for the sharper version. If the idea survives testing, name the gap it still doesn't cover and propose the most inventive fix you can find for it — a wedge, a feature, a channel, a business-model twist — not just a pass/fail stamp. If the test has already killed it (or clearly will), don't stop at red: use the same research to pin down exactly what killed it, then propose one concrete pivot that removes that specific flaw and would plausibly survive a retest. "It's dead" is never the final artifact — "here's the version that isn't" is. Then actually run that pivot back through the loop — see **Iterate** below; a pivot that only exists as a suggested paragraph hasn't been tested at all.
- **Ground the idea in real domain vocabulary, not invented placeholders.** Before finalizing FRAME or naming assumptions, check the idea against real industry/role/process knowledge instead of guessing generically — see [`references/domain-knowledge.md`](references/domain-knowledge.md). This is vocabulary only, never evidence of real-world conditions — see the mandatory internet-research rule below.

## The loop

Locate where the person is, run that stage, then push to the next. Do not run stages they are past.

0. **DISCOVER** *(only when there is no idea yet)* — If the person wants a market/pain found from scratch rather than an existing idea tested, run the wide-then-narrow funnel first: 10–20 candidate problem spaces → workflow-map the promising ones → market-map table → pain-score → shortlist ~5 → deep-dive the top 3 → pick one. Full process: [`references/discovery.md`](references/discovery.md). **Done when:** one opportunity is picked (with a stated second/third choice and why they lost), and it's handed to FRAME as the hypothesis to interrogate. Skip this stage entirely if they already brought an idea.

1. **FRAME** — Force the problem into focus before any solution. Answer, out loud, for their idea: *who exactly*, *what problem*, *why it happens*, *how often*, *how painful*, *what they use today*, *will they pay*. Vague answers here are the finding — chase them down; ground the customer/role/process in real domain knowledge rather than a generic guess ([`references/domain-knowledge.md`](references/domain-knowledge.md)). Full battery: [`references/framing.md`](references/framing.md). **Done when:** the idea is stated as one falsifiable hypothesis sentence naming the customer, the pain, and the willingness-to-pay.

2. **ASSUME** — List everything that must be true for this to work (customer has the problem / it is painful enough / they will pay $X / you can reach them profitably / you can deliver it / it can get large). Rank each by *risk × uncertainty*. Cross-check against the domain's real value chain ([`references/domain-knowledge.md`](references/domain-knowledge.md)) — an assumption that skips a layer of who-does-what-with-what is usually the unexamined one. **Done when:** the single riskiest assumption is named — the one that is both most damaging if false and least known today.

3. **RED TEAM** — Before spending a real test on it, attack the hypothesis as a skeptical investor trying to kill it: run the objection list, name the economic buyer/user/champion/blocker, classify existing evidence (VERIFIED FACT → UNKNOWN), state the falsification conditions ("what would prove this isn't worth pursuing"), and write the Bull Case / Bear Case / Kill Shot / What-would-change-our-mind. Full process: [`references/red-team.md`](references/red-team.md). **Done when:** all four of Bull/Bear/Kill-Shot/What-would-change-our-mind are written with specifics, and the falsification conditions are stated — this is the concrete form of "never stop at kill it," not satisfied by a vague risk list.

4. **TEST** — Do not open with an instruction to the person. First, run desk research *and* social listening yourself, in this turn: search Reddit, X, Facebook/community groups, App/Play Store and G2/Capterra reviews, and forums for this exact customer + problem in their own words. This internet research is **mandatory** for validating real-world conditions — Atlas grounding covers vocabulary, never market reality — and the bar is **at least 100 distinct sources read and understood** across desk research + social listening combined before TEST counts as done (see [`references/research.md`](references/research.md) for what counts and how to report the count/spread). Unprompted complaints and "how do I deal with X" threads settle most of what a first interview round exists to find — pain is real, how it's described, how urgent it is. State plainly what that search did and didn't settle, and which stage of the [PMF ladder](references/red-team.md) the evidence actually supports. Only what's left — specifically "will they pay this price for this offer" — needs a person-run test, and it should be the smallest one that can answer it: landing page + ad spend, pre-order/waitlist, concierge/manual MVP, pilot. Reach for open-ended interviews only if social listening found nothing usable, and even then scope it small (3–5 people, days not a week), with behavioral not hypothetical questions ([`references/research.md`](references/research.md)). Not a finished product. State the pass/fail signal *before* running it. **Done when:** the 100-source minimum is met (or the shortfall is explained), there is one test, its cost, its turnaround time, and the observable result that would confirm or refute the assumption — and it's the smallest test left after your own research, not the first one that came to mind. Stage map (BUILD→SELL→LEARN→SCALE) if they are further along: [`references/loop.md`](references/loop.md).

5. **BET** — Convert to one concrete Horizon-1 action for this week (e.g. "launch a landing page with two pricing tiers by Wednesday" or "interview 5 clinic owners by Friday" — whichever the TEST stage actually produced), while keeping the ambitious Horizon-3 vision as the North Star. Use the 7-day-execution-plan / first-customer-plan formats when the person is a solo/small builder starting from zero: [`references/loop.md`](references/loop.md). **Done when:** the person has a single next action with a deadline.

6. **CANVAS** — Once FRAME (and ideally ASSUME/TEST) have real material, produce a proper Business Model Canvas: 9 blocks, each pulled directly from what the loop already established, not invented fresh — see [`references/business-model-canvas.md`](references/business-model-canvas.md) for the block-by-block sourcing rules, what "generic vs. specific" looks like per block, and how to mark unvalidated blocks. Price Revenue Streams against the customer-economics cost-of-pain calculation ([`references/economics.md`](references/economics.md)), not a feature count. Skip this stage (or produce only a flagged draft) if FRAME alone isn't done yet — a canvas off a one-line pitch is fiction. Localize any global-research benchmarks back to the actual target market's pricing power, channels, payment habits, and regulation before they land in the canvas — a foreign data point calibrates, it doesn't get copied in verbatim (same reference file has the localization rules). **Done when:** all 9 blocks are filled with specifics traceable to FRAME/ASSUME/TEST/economics, internally consistent, and the least-validated block is named.

Close every session with seven artifacts: **the hypothesis**, **the riskiest assumption**, **the red team write-up** (Bull/Bear/Kill-Shot/what-would-change-our-mind + falsification conditions), **the cheapest test + its pass/fail signal**, **the one action this week**, **the sharper version** — if the idea is still alive, the gap it doesn't cover yet plus the most inventive fix for it ("no better angle found" is only valid after genuinely looking); if the test killed it (or clearly will), the exact cause of death plus one concrete pivot that removes it — and **the Business Model Canvas** (or an explicitly flagged draft if FRAME isn't done). If any is missing, the loop is not done.

## Iterate: this is a loop, not one pass

The person isn't selling a fixed product or service — they're selling **a validated solution to a problem**, and "validated" is the actual deliverable, not "one hypothesis tested." One FRAME→ASSUME→TEST→BET→CANVAS pass rarely produces a hypothesis that survives on the first try — most of the time TEST kills the assumption or CANVAS exposes a block that doesn't hold up. When that happens, don't stop at reporting it: **restart the loop at FRAME with the sharper version / pivot as the new hypothesis**, and run it through ASSUME→TEST→BET→CANVAS again. Carry forward research already done (don't re-run the same 100+ sources if the customer/domain didn't change) and only do fresh research for what the pivot actually changed (new segment, new channel, new price).

**Stop iterating when one of these is true, and say explicitly which:**
- A hypothesis's riskiest assumption survives TEST (real evidence — social listening or a true-primary result — supports it, not just "no better angle found"). This is the success exit: report it as validated and move to BET/CANVAS as the final round.
- **Three iterations** have run and none survived. Stop, report the full iteration history (what each hypothesis was, what killed each one, what was learned across all of them), and say plainly that no validated angle was found yet within this session — don't quietly keep spinning or fake a pass to end the loop.
- The riskiest remaining assumption is now **irreversible to test cheaply** (would require real capital, a long contract, or burning the only shot at this customer segment) — stop and flag that further iteration needs a different kind of decision, not another cheap test.
- The person explicitly says they want to commit and build on the current best hypothesis, even if unvalidated — respect that; don't keep re-litigating (see "When NOT to run this").

Each iteration's artifacts stay part of the record — don't discard round 1's findings when round 2 starts; a later pivot is informed by exactly what killed the earlier one.

### When 3 iterations fail: diagnose before deciding to pivot a 4th time or restart clean

Hitting the cap doesn't automatically mean "try one more pivot." Read the iteration history and ask: did each round die from a **different, unrelated** cause (bad channel, then bad price, then no urgency) — pivoting has genuinely been narrowing it down and one more targeted round could plausibly land it? Or did every round die from **the same underlying reason wearing a different costume** (this customer doesn't have the pain / can't be reached profitably / this whole problem space is a vitamin, not a painkiller) — in which case another pivot inside the same idea is polishing a dead thing, and it doesn't make sense to keep looping on it.

- **If the failures are related/systemic:** say so explicitly, name the shared root cause across all 3 rounds, and recommend **abandoning this problem/customer entirely and restarting from DISCOVER** ([`references/discovery.md`](references/discovery.md)) with a genuinely different problem or customer, not another variant of the same one — treat it as a fresh wide-funnel search (possibly in a different Atlas industry), not a FRAME-level tweak.
- **If the failures are genuinely distinct:** it's fair to propose one more targeted iteration, but say explicitly why this one is different from the first three (what specific new information makes it worth a 4th round) — don't spend a 4th round on inertia alone.
- Either way, this is a recommendation to state plainly, not a decision to make unilaterally — hand it back to the person as "here's what I'd do next and why" and let them choose.

## Research: desk, social listening, and true primary

Use **web search / browser tools** during OBSERVE, FRAME, and TEST to run two kinds of research yourself, before assigning anything to the person: **desk research** (existing solutions, competitors, market size, pricing, trends) and **social listening** (searching Reddit, X, community groups, App/Play Store and review-site complaints for this exact customer talking about this exact pain, unprompted, in their own words). Together these settle most of what a first interview round exists to find — do them in the same turn you're asked, not as a suggestion.

This internet research is **mandatory for seeing real conditions** — it is how the loop knows what's actually happening in the market, not an optional enrichment step. Atlas Domain Knowledge is a separate, narrower tool: pull it in only when the loop needs real business/domain vocabulary (role names, process steps, system types) it doesn't already have — it is never a stand-in for checking real conditions, and never counts toward the source minimum below.

**Minimum bar: at least 100 distinct websites/sources read and understood** (competitor pages, reviews, forum threads, marketplace listings, articles, social posts) across desk research + social listening combined, before TEST's research phase counts as done. State the count and spread when reporting back. Full detail: [`references/research.md`](references/research.md).

Search **globally, not just the local market or local language** — run both kinds of research in English and other relevant languages against worldwide sources, not only Indonesian ones, even for an Indonesia-first idea. A sharper wedge, a cheaper channel, or a cautionary already-tried solution can come from anywhere.

What's left has a hard ceiling neither search can reach: whether this customer will actually *pay* this price for this offer. That's the only thing that needs a person-run test — and it should be the smallest one that answers just that question, not a default week of interviews. How to run each well: [`references/research.md`](references/research.md).

## When NOT to run this

- They have already validated and committed, and are now building — help build, do not re-litigate the idea.
- The decision is small and reversible — just make the call and move on; this loop is for bets worth reasoning about.
